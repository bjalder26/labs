Electrochemical Cell Lab

Name:

Electrochemical Cells: Predicting Anodes, Cathodes, and Cell Potentials

Purpose

In this investigation, you will use standard reduction potentials to predict:

  • Which electrode will be the anode
  • Which electrode will be the cathode
  • The voltage produced by an electrochemical cell

You will then construct several electrochemical cells, measure their voltages, and compare your predictions to the experimental results.

Finally, you will investigate whether cell potentials are additive.

Background

An electrochemical (or galvanic) cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction.

Every electrochemical cell contains two electrodes:

  • Anode: the electrode where oxidation occurs
  • Cathode: the electrode where reduction occurs

Electrons are released at the anode and travel through the external wire to the cathode. Therefore:

Electrons flow from the anode to the cathode.

The flow of electrons through the wire is what produces an electric current.

Standard Reduction Potentials

Different substances have different tendencies to gain electrons. These tendencies are represented by their standard reduction potentials.

A larger (more positive) reduction potential means a species is more likely to be reduced.

The standard reduction potentials used in this lab are shown below.

Half-Cell Standard Reduction Potential, E° (V)
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu +0.34
Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb −0.13
Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe −0.44
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn −0.76

Determining the Anode and Cathode

To determine which electrode is the cathode and which is the anode:

  1. Compare the two reduction potentials.
  2. The electrode with the more positive reduction potential is the cathode.
  3. The electrode with the less positive (or more negative) reduction potential is the anode.

Example: Zn/Cu Cell

  • Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V
  • Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V

Since copper has the more positive reduction potential:

  • Copper is the cathode.
  • Zinc is the anode.

Calculating Cell Potential

The standard cell potential is calculated using:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Example: Zn/Cu Cell

E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76)
E°cell = 1.10 V

A positive cell potential indicates a spontaneous electrochemical cell.

Using the Voltmeter

After predicting the anode and cathode:

  • Connect the red lead of the voltmeter to the predicted cathode.
  • Connect the black lead of the voltmeter to the predicted anode.

If your prediction is correct, the voltmeter should display a positive voltage.

If the voltmeter displays a negative voltage:

  • Reverse the leads.
  • The electrode you connected to the red lead is the cathode.
  • The electrode you connected to the black lead is the anode.

The sign of the voltage can therefore be used to check your prediction.

Cell Potentials Are Additive

Because cell potentials are differences between electrode potentials, they can be added in certain situations.

EZn/Cu = EZn/Pb + EPb/Cu

You will test this relationship using your experimental data.

  1. Assemble the Zn/Cu electrochemical cell using the porous cup.
